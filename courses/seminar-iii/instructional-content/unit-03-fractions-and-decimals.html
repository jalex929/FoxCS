Seminar III · Unit 03 · Fractions & Decimals

Different Ways to Show the Same Amount

This week's big questions: What quantity does this number represent? and would a fraction or decimal be more useful here?

Session A: Fractions

Meaning, equivalence, comparison, and operations.

What Does a Fraction Mean?

A fraction should not be treated as just two numbers separated by a line. A fraction can represent part of a whole, part of a group, a point on a number line, or division.

3/4  →  one whole has been divided into four equal parts, and three of those parts are represented.
  • Numerator: the top number. It tells how many selected parts are being represented.
  • Denominator: the bottom number. It tells how many equal parts make one whole.

A fraction also means division. 3/4 also means 3 ÷ 4, which equals 0.75. This connection matters when converting fractions to decimals later this week.

Fractions are also points on a number line, not only "pieces of something." 1/2 is a point halfway between 0 and 1. 3/2 is greater than 1, located halfway between 1 and 2.

Common Misconception Fractions do not have to be less than 1. 5/4, 7/3, and 10/5 are legitimate values, not "wrong" answers that need fixing.

Equivalent Fractions and Simplifying

Equivalent fractions look different but represent the same quantity.

1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 = 5/10

If both the numerator and denominator are multiplied by the same nonzero number, the size of the quantity does not change.

  1. Start with 1/2.
  2. Multiply numerator and denominator by 3: (1 × 3)/(2 × 3) = 3/6.
  3. 1/2 and 3/6 represent the same quantity.

Simplifying rewrites a fraction using smaller whole numbers. 12/18 is divisible by 6 in both places: 12 ÷ 6 = 2 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3, so 12/18 = 2/3.

Common Misconception Simplifying does not make a fraction smaller. 6/8 = 3/4. The representation changes. The quantity does not.

Comparing Fractions

No single strategy works best for every comparison. Choose the one that fits the problem.

same denominatorsame numeratorcommon denominatorconvert to decimalbenchmarks
  • Same denominator: 3/8 and 5/8 have equal-size pieces. Five eighths contains more pieces, so 5/8 > 3/8.
  • Same numerator: 3/4 and 3/8 both have three pieces, but fourths are larger than eighths, so 3/4 > 3/8.
  • Common denominator: to compare 3/4 and 5/8, convert 3/4 to 6/8. Since 6/8 > 5/8, 3/4 > 5/8.
  • Convert to decimal: 3/4 = 0.75 and 5/8 = 0.625, so 3/4 > 5/8.
  • Benchmarks: 7/10 is greater than 1/2, and 3/8 is less than 1/2, so 7/10 > 3/8. This strategy can be faster than calculating.
Common Misconception A larger denominator does not mean a larger fraction. 1/8 is not greater than 1/4. More equal parts means each piece is smaller: 1/8 < 1/4.

Adding and Subtracting Fractions

With the same denominator, the pieces are already the same size. Add or subtract the number of pieces and keep the denominator.

1/5 + 2/5  →  add the pieces: 1 + 2 = 3  →  keep the denominator: 3/5
Common Mistake 1/5 + 2/5 ≠ 3/10. Adding the denominators would incorrectly change the size of the pieces. The operation combines fifths. It does not turn fifths into tenths.

With different denominators, the pieces are different sizes. Create equivalent fractions with same-size pieces before adding or subtracting.

  1. 1/2 + 1/4: the pieces are different sizes.
  2. Convert: 1/2 = 2/4.
  3. Add: 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.

Subtraction works the same way. 5/6 - 1/3: convert 1/3 to 2/6, then 5/6 - 2/6 = 3/6, which simplifies to 1/2.

Why does this matter conceptually? If one person has 1/2 of a pizza and another has 1/4 of the same pizza, you cannot say "one piece plus one piece equals two pieces" without noticing the pieces are different sizes. A common denominator rewrites the quantities using equal-size pieces.

Multiplying and Dividing Fractions

Fraction multiplication means part of a part. 1/2 × 3/4 means one-half of three-fourths.

1/2 × 3/4  →  multiply numerators: 1 × 3 = 3  →  multiply denominators: 2 × 4 = 8  →  result: 3/8

Multiplication does not require a common denominator. 2/3 × 4/5 = (2 × 4)/(3 × 5) = 8/15.

Reasonableness Check Both values in 1/2 × 3/4 are positive and less than 1. Taking part of a number less than 1 should produce something smaller. An answer such as 6/4 should immediately look unreasonable.

Division asks a different question. 3/4 ÷ 1/2 asks: how many groups of one-half fit into three-fourths? The answer is 3/2, or 1 1/2.

  1. Keep the first fraction: 3/4.
  2. Change division to multiplication.
  3. Use the reciprocal of the second fraction: 1/2 becomes 2/1.
  4. Multiply: 3/4 × 2/1 = 6/4, which simplifies to 3/2.

Learners may use the reciprocal procedure ("keep, change, flip"), but should first ask what the original division represents rather than relying on the shortcut alone.

Mixed Numbers and Improper Fractions

  • Proper fraction: numerator is smaller than the denominator. Example: 3/5.
  • Improper fraction: numerator is equal to or greater than the denominator. Example: 7/4.
  • Mixed number: a whole number combined with a fraction. Example: 1 3/4.

7/4 = 1 3/4. Being able to recognize and interpret these forms matters more this week than heavy mixed-number arithmetic.

Session B: Decimals

Place value, operations, and connecting decimals back to fractions.

Decimal Place Value

Each digit in a decimal represents a specific place value.

4.583  →  4 ones, 5 tenths, 8 hundredths, 3 thousandths
ones = 1tenths = 0.1hundredths = 0.01thousandths = 0.001

In 4.583, the 8 represents 0.08, because it sits in the hundredths place.

Trailing zeros added to the right of a decimal do not change its value: 0.5 = 0.50 = 0.500. This is useful when comparing decimals.

Comparing Decimals

Compare digits according to place value, not according to how many digits are present.

  1. Compare 0.58 and 0.6.
  2. Rewrite with the same number of places: 0.58 and 0.60.
  3. Compare tenths first: 0.60 > 0.58.
  4. Therefore 0.6 > 0.58.
Common Misconception 0.58 is not greater than 0.6 just because 58 is greater than 6. Place value decides, not digit count. 0.6 means six tenths. 0.58 means fifty-eight hundredths. Since 0.60 > 0.58, 0.6 > 0.58.

Decimal Operations

Addition and subtraction: line up the decimal points so digits with the same place value are combined.

4.75 + 2.60 = 7.35
8.20 − 3.47 = 4.73

Multiplication: 2.5 × 4 can be reasoned as 2.5 + 2.5 + 2.5 + 2.5 = 10. For more complex multiplication, a calculator may be appropriate, but estimate first or afterward.

Estimate 4.9 × 3.1  →  round to 5 × 3  →  ≈ 15. An exact result should land near 15. An answer such as 151.9 should immediately look wrong.

Division: interpret the meaning in context. $12.60 divided equally among 3 people is 12.60 ÷ 3 = $4.20 each.

Common Misconception Correct digits do not guarantee a correct answer if the decimal point is placed incorrectly. 2.4 × 3 answered as 72 should immediately look wrong: 2 × 3 ≈ 6.

Fractions and Decimals Are Connected

A fraction represents division, so any fraction can be converted to a decimal by dividing.

3/4  →  3 ÷ 4 = 0.75

A terminating decimal can be converted back to a fraction using place value.

  1. 0.6 means six tenths: 6/10.
  2. Simplify: 6/10 = 3/5.

Learners should gradually become fluent with common benchmarks:

1/2 = 0.51/4 = 0.253/4 = 0.751/5 = 0.22/5 = 0.43/5 = 0.64/5 = 0.81/10 = 0.1

These benchmarks will be especially important next week, when percent is introduced.

Choosing a Useful Representation

The same quantity can often be shown in more than one way. 3/4 and 0.75 are the same number. One form may simply be easier in a given context.

  • Money: a price is usually written as $2.75, not $2 3/4, because decimals match how money is normally written.
  • Measurement: a recipe usually calls for 3/4 cup rather than 0.75 cup, because that form is more familiar in the kitchen.
  • Comparison: to compare 7/8 and 0.82, converting 7/8 to 0.875 makes the comparison immediate: 0.875 > 0.82.

The key idea: the representation can change while the value stays the same.

Does My Answer Make Sense?

Continue using Operation → Size → Sign → Unit → Context. For fractions and decimals, size becomes especially important.

OperationDid I choose an operation that matches what the situation actually asks?
SizeShould this answer be greater than 1 or less than 1? Should multiplying make the quantity smaller?
SignDoes the sign of the result make sense for the situation?
UnitAm I answering in the unit the question actually asked for (dollars, cups, miles)?
ContextIs this decimal close to the benchmark I expected? Does the result have a reasonable size for this situation?

Example: a learner calculates 1/2 × 1/3 = 5/6. One-half of one-third must be smaller than one-third, but 5/6 is much larger. The answer cannot be reasonable. Example: a learner calculates 4.7 + 3.2 = 79. Estimating gives 5 + 3 = 8, and 79 is nowhere near 8, which suggests a decimal-place or entry error.

Learn From the Error

Not every wrong answer means the same thing. Use the five error types from Unit 01.

Knowledge: "I don't remember how to add fractions with different denominators." Response: review common denominators and work through an example.
Process: knowing a common denominator is needed, but changing the denominator without changing the numerator. Response: compare the work with an equivalent-fraction example.
Execution: correctly setting up 0.74 + 1.26, then making an arithmetic mistake. Response: recalculate and estimate to verify.
Comprehension: a recipe uses 3/4 cup of milk and asks how much remains from 1 cup, but the learner reports 3/4. Response: reread what the question actually asks.
Strategy: doing complicated common-denominator work to compare 1/2 and 0.45 instead of recognizing 1/2 = 0.5. Response: choose a more useful representation.
