Guided Practice

Using the Five-Question Routine

Worked example: chore times 30+45+20=95 min, fits inside 120 min, enough time with 25 min to spare.

  1. $18 × 24 = $432. The budget is $350. Not enough. The trip costs $82 more than the budget.
  2. 3/4 cup × 2 batches = 1 1/2 cups of sugar.
  3. 25 + 15 = 40 minutes needed before 5:00 PM. Start getting ready at 4:20 PM.

Classifying the Error

Worked example: 9 − 4 + 2 solved as 4+2 first. Process Error (right numbers, wrong order).

  1. Comprehension Error. The question asked for the difference ("how many more"), but the student found the total instead.
  2. Knowledge Error. The student doesn't know or remember how to find a percent of a number.
  3. Execution Error. 6 × 7 = 42, not 48. The setup was right; the calculation itself slipped.
  4. Strategy Error. The approach worked but was far slower than testing the four answer choices.
  5. Knowledge Error. −6 + 9 = 3 (different signs, subtract magnitudes, keep the sign of the larger one). Getting −15 suggests the student applied the same-sign addition rule (adding magnitudes) instead of the different-signs rule, a rule they don't currently have down.

Independent Practice

Apply the Five-Question Routine

  1. Discount = 0.20 × $64 = $12.80. Sale price = $64 − $12.80 = $51.20. Yes, $55 is enough, with $3.80 to spare.
  2. Available time = 45 min × 6 days = 270 minutes. Project takes about 240 minutes (4 hours). Yes, that's enough time, with about 30 minutes to spare, as long as all six days are actually used.
  3. Station A: $3.29 × 10 = $32.90. Station B: $3.15 × 10 = $31.50. Station B saves $1.40 on the gas itself. The problem doesn't give enough information (extra distance, car's exact mileage cost) to precisely factor in the detour, so a complete answer should note that limitation rather than ignore it.

Classify the Error

  1. Knowledge Error. Doesn't know how to compare negative numbers.
  2. Knowledge Error. Doesn't know the vocabulary term.
  3. Process Error. Ignored the parentheses/order of operations; correct is 2 × 7 = 14.
  4. Process Error. Performed the steps in the wrong order.
  5. Execution Error. Correct setup, calculator entry mistake.
  6. Execution Error. Correct answer, copied incorrectly (transposed digits).
  7. Comprehension Error. Answered a different question than the one asked.
  8. Comprehension Error. Misread the graph's label.
  9. Strategy Error. A working but inefficient approach; estimating first would catch obvious errors faster.
  10. Strategy Error. A slower approach when a faster one (testing choices) was available.

Check

  1. B. Identify what the problem is asking before choosing a calculation.
  2. B. It checks whether the result actually fits the problem.
  3. A. Knowledge. A rule or fact the student doesn't currently know.
  4. B. Execution. Right process, a small slip carrying it out.
  5. C. Comprehension. Answered the wrong question (amount spent, not amount remaining).
  6. B. Process. Right idea, steps out of order.
  7. D. Strategy. A working but inefficient approach.
  8. B. Review and practice the underlying skill directly.
  9. C. Build a specific checking habit that targets the actual mistake.
  10. C. It identifies current strengths and areas for growth, not a grade or a ranking.
