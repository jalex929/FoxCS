Fractions & Decimals

Two ways to write the exact same amount. Once you can move between them, a lot of math gets easier.

Session A

Fractions: Meaning to Operations

What a fraction means · comparing them · adding, multiplying, dividing

What Is a Fraction?

Look at 3/4.

The bottom number, 4, is the denominator — how many equal parts one whole has been divided into. The top number, 3, is the numerator — how many of those parts you have. A fraction also means division: 3/4 also means 3÷4, which is 0.75.

Fractions Are Points, Not Just Pieces

Fractions don't have to be less than 1.

One half sits exactly halfway between 0 and 1. Three halves is greater than 1 — it sits halfway between 1 and 2.

Equivalent Fractions

Same amount, different name.

Multiply the numerator and denominator by the same nonzero number and the value doesn't change — the shaded amount is identical in all three bars.

Simplifying Fractions

12/18 simplifies to 2/3.

Both numbers divide evenly by 6: 12÷6=2, 18÷6=3. The way it's written changed. The quantity did not.

Which Fraction Is Bigger?

More pieces means smaller pieces — 1/8 is less than 1/4, even though 8 is a bigger number than 4. When denominators match, just compare numerators. When they don't, convert to decimals or use a benchmark like 1/2.

Adding Fractions

Same-size pieces first.

1/2 + 1/3 ≠ 2/5. The pieces have to be rewritten with a common denominator before you can add them.

Like denominators: add numerators directly, 1/5+2/5=3/5. Unlike denominators: find a common denominator first, 1/2+1/4 → 2/4+1/4=3/4.

Multiplying & Dividing Fractions

Multiplying means "part of a part."

1/2 × 3/4 means half of three fourths — multiply straight across: 1×3=3, 2×4=8, so 3/8. No common denominator needed; that rule is only for addition and subtraction.

Dividing asks a different question: 3/4 ÷ 1/2 asks how many halves fit into 3/4 — the answer is 1½. Shortcut: keep the first fraction, flip the second, multiply.

Session B

Decimals & Choosing a Representation

Decimal place value and operations · fractions and decimals as the same number, different form

Understanding Decimals

Every digit after the decimal point has a specific place value.

In 4.583, the 5 is tenths, the 8 is hundredths, the 3 is thousandths — that 8 represents 0.08, not just "the number 8." Adding zeros doesn't change the value: 0.5 = 0.50 = 0.500.

Is 0.58 greater than 0.6, because 58 looks bigger than 6? No — rewrite with matching digits: 0.60 vs 0.58. Place value decides, not digit count.

Decimal Operations

Adding or subtracting: line up the decimal points so you're combining digits with the same place value.

Multiplying, especially with a calculator: always estimate first. 4.9 × 3.1 should land close to 5×3=15. If your calculator shows 151.9, something went wrong — probably a decimal point in the wrong place.

Fractions and Decimals Are Connected

Since a fraction means division, convert a fraction to a decimal by dividing: 3/4 = 3÷4 = 0.75. Going the other way, a decimal becomes a fraction using its place value: 0.6 = six tenths = 6/10 = 3/5.

1/2 = 0.51/4 = 0.253/4 = 0.75 1/5 = 0.22/5 = 0.41/10 = 0.1

These benchmarks come up constantly — they're worth knowing cold. We'll use them again next week for percent.

Which Form Should I Use?

Money is almost always a decimal: $2.75, not $2¾. Recipes usually use fractions: 3/4 cup, not 0.75 cup. When comparing two values, decimals can make it easier: converting 7/8 to 0.875 makes it immediately clear that 7/8 > 0.82.
