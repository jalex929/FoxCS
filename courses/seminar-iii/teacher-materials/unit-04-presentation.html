Session A

Percent: Meaning & Structure

Percent as out of 100 · fraction ↔ decimal ↔ percent · benchmarks · find the part, the percent, or the whole

Percent Means Out of 100

Percent comes from "per hundred."

25% = 25/100 = 0.25 = 1/4
Percent is not a new kind of number — it is a new way of writing a quantity students already understand as a fraction or decimal.

Same Number, Different Form

Fraction ↔ Decimal ↔ Percent

1/2 = 0.5 = 50% 1/4 = 0.25 = 25% 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%
Unit 04 builds directly on Unit 03's fraction-decimal work rather than introducing percent as unrelated content.

Common Misconception

Is 20% the same as 20?

No. 20% = 0.20, or 20/100. Treating the percent number as a whole number skips the "out of 100" meaning entirely.

Common Misconception

Does 35% become 3.5?

No — it becomes 0.35.

Reasonableness catches this: 35% is less than 100%, so its decimal form must be less than 1.

Benchmark Percentages

Useful reference points for estimating.

10% = 1/10 25% = 1/4 50% = 1/2 75% = 3/4
Mental strategy chain: 10% of a number → divide by 10. 1% → divide by 100. 5% is half of 10%. 20% is double 10%. Build less familiar percents from these.

Percent Is Not Limited to 0-100

200% means twice the original amount.

50% = half 100% = the whole amount 200% = double
Students should not assume percentages must always fall between 0% and 100%.

The Three Major Percent Questions

Which quantity is missing?

Find the Part Find the Percent Find the Whole
Understanding which quantity is unknown matters more than memorizing three separate formulas.

Type 1 — Find the Part

What is 30% of 80?

30% = 0.30  →  0.30 × 80 = 24
The whole and percent are known. The part is unknown.

Type 2 — Find the Percent

18 out of 24 students completed an assignment. What percent?

18 ÷ 24 = 0.75  →  75%
The part and whole are known. The percent is unknown. Setup: part ÷ whole, then convert to percent.

Type 3 — Find the Whole

$15 is 25% of what amount?

25% = 1/4  →  15 × 4 = 60
The part and percent are known. The whole is unknown. Benchmark reasoning or dividing by the decimal percent both work.

Common Misconception

18 of 24 students — which number goes on top?

Incorrect: 24 ÷ 18. Correct: 18 ÷ 24. Identify the part and the whole before calculating, not after.

Exit Ticket — Session A

  1. Convert a decimal to a percent
  2. Convert a percent to a decimal
  3. Convert a fraction to a percent

Session B

Percent in Money & Change

Discounts, tax, tips · percent increase and decrease · apply, analyze, retry, and the Unit 04 check

One of the Most Important Skills This Week

What is the complete starting amount?

Discount → original price Test score → total points Survey → total students Percent change → original value
A student who identifies the wrong whole can perform an otherwise correct calculation and still get the wrong answer.

Discounts

A $70 jacket is 20% off.

Discount: 0.20 × 70 = $14  ·  Sale price: 70 − 14 = $56
A discount reduces an original price. Find the discount, then subtract it.

Common Misconception

Is the discount amount the same as the sale price?

No. "How much is the discount?" and "What is the sale price?" are two different questions with two different answers — $14 vs. $56 in the jacket example.

Sales Tax

A $40 purchase has 10% tax.

Tax: 0.10 × 40 = $4  ·  Total: 40 + 4 = $44
Tax increases the amount owed. Find the tax, then add it.

Common Misconception

Should tax ever be subtracted?

No — tax increases the amount owed.

A $50 purchase with $5 tax totals $55, not $45.

Estimating Tips

A meal costs $36. Estimate a 20% tip.

10% of $36 = $3.60  →  double it: 20% ≈ $7.20
The 10%-then-adjust strategy works for most tip percentages without exact decimal multiplication.

Percent Increase & Decrease

Compare the change to the original value, not the new value.

50 → 60: 10 ÷ 50 = 20% increase 80 → 60: 20 ÷ 80 = 25% decrease
Ask: how large was the change compared with where I started?

Common Misconception

Does a 20% increase then a 20% decrease return to the start?

$100 → +20% → $120 → −20% → $96, not $100. The second 20% is calculated from a different, larger whole.

Does My Answer Make Sense?

Operation → Size → Direction → Unit → Context

  • Is the percent less than 100%? Should the part be smaller than the whole?
  • Is this a discount or an increase — should the amount go up or down?
  • Did I answer with the percent amount or the final amount?

Apply, Analyze, Retry

Mixed practice, error analysis, and the Unit 04 check

  • Mixed retrieval: conversions, part/percent/whole, discounts, tax, tips, percent change
  • Students classify a missed item by error type, then retry after feedback
  • Unit 04 check, then reflection: one percent skill for outside school, one skill needing practice, one error made, one checking strategy

Exit Ticket — Session B

  1. Solve one discount or tax problem
  2. Report both the percent amount and the final total
  3. Explain which value you used as the whole
