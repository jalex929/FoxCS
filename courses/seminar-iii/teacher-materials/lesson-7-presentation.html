Session A

One-Step and Two-Step Equations

What an equation claims, the balance idea, inverse operations, and solving one-step and two-step equations

What Is an Equation?

Two expressions that claim to be equal.

x + 4 = 10
An equation makes a specific claim about a value. An expression (Lesson 6) has no equal sign and makes no claim.

The Balance Idea

Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.

x + 4 = 10  →  subtract 4 from both sides  →  x = 6
The balance stays true only when the exact same operation is applied to both sides.

Common Misconception

Does only one side need to change?

"If 4 is subtracted from the left side, the right side stays the same."

Not correct. The same operation must be applied to both sides, or the equation is no longer balanced.

Inverse Operations

What's being done to the variable, and what undoes it?

Addition ↔ SubtractionMultiplication ↔ Division
Ask this question before choosing a step: what operation is currently applied to the variable?

One-Step Equations: Addition

x + 7 = 12  →  subtract 7 from both sides  →  x = 5
7 is being added to x. The inverse operation is subtraction.

One-Step Equations: Subtraction

x − 9 = 4  →  add 9 to both sides  →  x = 13
9 is being subtracted from x. The inverse operation is addition.

One-Step Equations: Multiplication

5x = 35  →  divide both sides by 5  →  x = 7
x is being multiplied by 5. The inverse operation is division.

One-Step Equations: Division

x/4 = 9  →  multiply both sides by 4  →  x = 36
x is being divided by 4. The inverse operation is multiplication.

Common Misconception

Can any operation isolate the variable?

"To solve x + 7 = 12, multiply both sides by something."

The operation used should be the inverse of whatever is currently applied to the variable. Since 7 is added, subtraction undoes it, not multiplication.

Solving a Two-Step Equation

Undo in reverse order.

3x + 5 = 20  →  subtract 5: 3x = 15  →  divide by 3: x = 5
x is first multiplied by 3, then 5 is added. Undo the addition first, then the multiplication.

Why the Order Matters

Undo addition/subtraction before multiplication/division.

Harder path: dividing 3x + 5 = 20 by 3 first gives x + 5/3 = 20/3. Mathematically valid, but far more error-prone.
Undoing in reverse order keeps every step simple and avoids unnecessary fractions.

Two-Step Equation With Subtraction

4x − 6 = 18  →  add 6: 4x = 24  →  divide by 4: x = 6

Two-Step Equation With a Negative Coefficient

−2x + 9 = 1  →  subtract 9: −2x = −8  →  divide by −2: x = 4
Remember Lesson 2's sign rules: a negative divided by a negative is positive.

Common Misconception

Do negative coefficients need a different method?

"Equations with a negative coefficient require a different method."

The same steps apply. Dividing by a negative number just calls on the Lesson 2 sign rules.

Exit Ticket · Session A

  1. Solve a one-step addition/subtraction equation
  2. Solve a one-step multiplication/division equation
  3. Identify the inverse operation needed for a given equation

Session B

Combining, Distributing, Checking, and Context

Equations that need a setup step first, checking a solution, and equations built from real situations

Combining Like Terms First

2x + 3x − 4 = 16  →  combine: 5x − 4 = 16  →  add 4, divide 5: x = 4
Combine like terms on the same side before applying inverse operations.

The Distributive Property First

3(x + 2) = 21  →  distribute: 3x + 6 = 21  →  subtract 6, divide 3: x = 5

Combined Example

2(x + 4) + x = 20  →  distribute: 2x + 8 + x = 20  →  combine: 3x + 8 = 20  →  x = 4
Distribute, then combine like terms, then isolate the variable.

Common Misconception

Is distributing optional?

"3(x + 2) = 21 can be solved by dividing by 3 and subtracting 2, without distributing."

That alternate path only works if every term is divided consistently. Distributing first is more reliable, especially with extra terms outside the parentheses.

Common Misconception

Does combining like terms change the equation?

"Combining 2x + 3x into 5x changes what the equation represents."

Combining like terms simplifies the equation without changing its meaning or its solution.

Checking a Solution

Substitute back into the original equation.

3x + 5 = 20, solved as x = 5  →  3(5) + 5 = 20  →  20 = 20
Substitute into the original equation, not a simplified version, to confirm the solution.

Why Checking Matters

Checking catches solving mistakes.

4x − 3 = 13, solved as x = 4  →  4(4) − 3 = 13  →  13 = 13
If the check had failed, the student would know to review their steps rather than assume the answer is correct.

Common Misconception

Does checking mean solving again?

"Checking a solution means solving the equation again from scratch."

Checking means substituting the solution into the original equation and confirming both sides are equal. A different process from solving.

Equations in Context

Translate the situation, then solve.

$20 flat fee + $6/hour, total $56  →  6h + 20 = 56  →  h = 6
A rental company charges a $20 flat fee plus $6 per hour. A customer's total bill was $56. How many hours did the customer rent the equipment?

Interpreting the Solution

A number is not automatically the final answer.

h = 6 means the customer rented the equipment for 6 hours, not that the answer is simply "6." State the answer with the correct unit and context.

Common Misconception

Is finding x always the finish line?

"Once x is found, the word problem is finished."

The value of x must be interpreted in context, including its unit and meaning, before it becomes the final answer.

Does My Answer Make Sense?

SizeSignSubstitution CheckContext
Is the solution a realistic size? Should it be positive, negative, or zero? Does it check? Does it make sense in the situation?

Reasonableness Example

6h + 20 = 56, solved as h = −6.

A negative number of hours does not make sense in this context. The result signals a likely sign error, and the work should be reviewed.

Exit Ticket · Session B

  1. Check a given solution by substitution
  2. Translate a short scenario into an equation and solve it
  3. State the solution with the correct unit and context
