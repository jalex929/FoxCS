0.8 — Academic Integrity: Doing Your Own Work

What you'll take away:
  • I can explain why doing my own work matters for my own learning, not just for a rule's sake.
  • I can describe what counts as real help versus what crosses a line, for both AI tools and classmates.
  • I know exactly what happens if that line gets crossed, and why the honest attempt is always the better outcome.

This lesson gets its own space in the course, not just a quick mention, because it matters that much. Read it carefully. All of it applies to this class, no matter what you're building.

Why This Actually Matters

You will not learn if you do not attempt the work yourself. That's not a rule, it's just true. This material will be genuinely challenging at times. You're learning something entirely new, and for much of it, you're learning a new language for telling a computer exactly what to do. That difficulty is expected. It's not a sign something has gone wrong, it's what learning something real looks like.

Being Present Matters

Game Programming I, Game Programming II, and Web Design II all cover a lot of ground. Being here to experience it firsthand, and to ask questions in the moment, isn't optional if you want to actually keep up. There isn't really a substitute for it.

Partner Work

When an assignment is partner work, partners work together. Both people actually doing the work, not one person doing it while the other watches.

If a partnership breaks down and one person ends up doing all or almost all of the work alone, here's exactly what happens: only the partner who did the work gets credit for it. The other partner doesn't receive credit for work they didn't do, and will be expected to restart the assignment on their own. This isn't a surprise rule, it's stated here plainly so everyone knows the expectation from day one.

Helping a Classmate the Right Way

There should never be a situation where you ask a classmate for their code, or any other work, to copy and paste. Not "just to see it," not "just this once." That line doesn't move.

If a classmate asks you for help, here's the difference between actually helping and just enabling a shortcut:

Not helping
Handing over your code or your answer so they can submit it as their own.
Actually helping
Explaining how to think through the problem. Pointing at the concept or the specific line that's off. Helping them find their own mistake.

This matters: if you hand someone your work to copy, you're not being generous, you're helping them cheat, and it carries the same consequence as cheating does (see below). This applies to both people involved, not just the one who copied.

Using AI Tools

To start the year, we will not be using AI in our work. The starting rule for this class: AI does not do your work for you. Not your code, not your written responses, not any part of an assignment you submit as your own.

A note about the future: later in the year, once you've built real foundational skills without AI, there may be specific situations where using it is allowed, to help you break down a problem, check your own work, or explain something you didn't understand. If that happens, your teacher will state exactly when it's allowed and exactly what you're allowed to use it for. Until you hear that explicitly, for a specific assignment, the answer is no. AI helping you understand something is a very different thing from AI doing the thing for you, and that second one is never allowed, at any point in the year, in any form.

If This Line Gets Crossed

This applies to submitting AI-generated work as your own, copying a classmate's code or written work, and enabling someone else to do the same, for every person involved, not just whoever benefited most visibly:

  1. A call or email home.
  2. A written record of the incident, logged in Aspen, which leads to a conversation with a member of the school's Disciplinary Team.
  3. A 0% on the assignment, which cannot be made up.

Why Trying Honestly Is Always the Better Choice

Genuine effort always earns credit, even when the work is incomplete or wrong. A real, honest attempt that doesn't fully work is worth more than a submission that skipped the effort entirely, because effort is part of what's actually being graded here, not just the final answer. Using AI to do your work for you shows no effort at all, and earns none.

Put plainly: even when you don't get it right, trying it yourself will always earn you more points than cheating would. That's true on purpose. It's the whole point of how this is set up.
