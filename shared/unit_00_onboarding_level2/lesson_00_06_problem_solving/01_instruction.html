Thinking Like a Builder

0.6 — How Problem-Solving Works

What you'll take away:
  • I can describe a repeatable process for solving a problem I don't already know how to solve.

The last lesson introduced computational thinking, the mental habits behind breaking problems apart. This lesson is about what to actually do, step by step, when you're facing a problem you don't yet know how to solve. This process works no matter what you're building.

1Understand the problem

Before doing anything, be able to say, in your own words, what you're actually trying to accomplish. What should the end result look like or do? If you can't describe that clearly, that's the real first problem to solve.

2Plan before you build

Sketch out your approach, even roughly, before diving in. This could be a few written steps, a quick drawing, or just talking through it out loud. A little planning up front saves a lot of backtracking later.

3Try the smallest useful version first

Don't try to build the whole thing at once. Build the smallest piece that lets you check whether your basic idea works, then build outward from there.

4Check your work against what you expected

Compare what actually happened to what you planned in Step 1. If they match, great, move on. If they don't, that's not failure, that's information. Go back to troubleshooting (Lesson 0.4) to figure out why.

5Revise

Adjust your approach based on what you learned, and try again. Most real solutions go through more than one version before they're right.

This Isn't Just for Code

This same five-step process works for debugging a program, figuring out why something doesn't display correctly, or working out why a game isn't behaving the way you designed it. But it's not only for building things, either. It's the same process you'd use to figure out why a recipe didn't turn out right, why directions to a friend's house led somewhere wrong, or how to fix a schedule that suddenly has too much packed into one afternoon. The tools are different every time, the process is the same.

You'll come back to this process constantly this year, not just when something goes wrong, but as a normal way to approach any new problem from the start.
