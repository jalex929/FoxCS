0.7 — Getting Unstuck

What you'll take away:
  • I can list several ways to get unstuck before asking for help.
  • I can ask for help in a way that actually builds my own understanding, instead of skipping past it.

Everyone gets stuck this year. That's not a problem to avoid, it's a normal part of learning something new, exactly like the last few lessons already covered. This lesson is about what to actually do about it.

Try These Before You Ask Anyone

Re-read what you wrote
Slowly, out loud if it helps. A surprising number of problems are visible the second time you actually look closely, instead of skimming.
Re-read the error or symptom
What is it actually telling you? Not what you assume it means, what it literally says.
Check the instructions or documentation again
It's easy to misremember a detail. Going back to the source is faster than guessing.
Try isolating the problem
Comment out or remove pieces until you find the smallest version that still breaks. That tells you exactly where to look.

If you've genuinely tried these and you're still stuck, that's exactly when to ask for help. Asking isn't a last resort you should feel bad about, it's the right next step once you've done your own thinking first.

How to Ask for Help Well

The way you ask for help changes how much you actually learn from it.

Less helpful
"It's not working." (No detail. Whoever's helping you has to start from zero.)
More helpful
"I expected X to happen, but Y happened instead. Here's what I tried already. Here's the exact error message I'm seeing."

The second version does two things: it respects the other person's time, and it forces you to actually think through the problem yourself first, which often helps you notice the answer before you even finish asking the question.

Who to Ask

Asking a classmate is a great first option, often the fastest one, and it's genuinely encouraged in this class. The key is knowing how to ask well, using exactly the approach above: what you expected, what happened, what you already tried. Your teacher is always available too, especially for anything a classmate can't resolve.

There's an important line about what "helping" actually means when it comes to someone else's code or work. The next lesson covers that boundary directly, since it matters enough to get its own real space rather than a quick mention here.
